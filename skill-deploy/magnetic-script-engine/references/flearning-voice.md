# F.Learning voice: clear, human, playfully clever

This is the user's requested creative direction, informed by F.Learning's public
animation examples, not a verbatim official brand manual. Apply it across ICPs and
narrative styles. Aim for a capable, friendly storyteller with a little mischief:
precise knowledge, lively delivery and animation that has a personality.

## Put the playfulness in the explanation

Look for an opportunity to make the idea click with a small surprise: an object
with an attitude, a visual double take, a dry aside, a witty turn of phrase, a
match cut, an accumulating visual joke or a callback that lands differently at the
end. Choose a device that belongs to the source's task and assigned style. No joke
count, obligatory mascot, slang quota or fixed beat pattern applies.

Let voiceover and picture play off each other. The voice can stay straight while
the image supplies the wink; a source-listed checklist might make a modest bow at
its review, or an oversized question mark might shrink when the next step becomes
clear. Such motion is a visual convention, not a claim that an outcome occurred.
Give the device a role in the explanation, then move on. Repeated captions, random
sound effects and generic cheerfulness are not the same as a playful idea.

Write with contractions, varied cadence and confident, conversational turns. A
little understatement or a short aside can work better than announcing excitement.
Do not explain the joke or say the script is “fun”, “funky” or “engaging”. Ordinary
read-aloud instructions should become a scene, relationship or image worth following.

## Original craft examples

These are fictional craft demonstrations, not facts for another document.

Source: “The handbook explains working hours and leave requests.”
Flat: “The handbook contains information about working hours and leave requests.”
Playful option: “Working hours? Leave requests? The handbook has those covered.”
Visual: two question marks become tabs on the handbook; it closes with a small bow.
The line does not claim the handbook answers every possible question.

Source: “During the first week, complete the checklist and review it with your manager.”
Playful option: “That checklist has a first-week appointment: a review with your manager.”
Visual: the checklist slides into the review scene as “During the first week” stays
visible. Keep the actual completion instruction in the surrounding narration; the
metaphor must not add a booked meeting or a new deadline.

The supplied cirrhosis example's object narrator demonstrates attitude and comic
reversal; the HOPES pair demonstrates setup and callback. Borrow these techniques,
not their clinical claims, fictional histories or specific punchlines.

## Match the emotional context

For ordinary learning and onboarding, allow warmth, wit, expressive objects and
bolder transitions. For healthcare, let playfulness make a concept approachable,
then speak plainly at doses, thresholds, symptoms and urgent action instructions.
Sensitive experiences may call for warm observation rather than a joke. Never make
a person's illness, disability, distress, culture, literacy or mistake the target.

Personification may give an illustrated object a theatrical voice, but it must not
teach new biological intent, causation, risk or treatment effects. Preserve facts,
conditions and locked instructions. Do not soften urgency, invent reassurance or
promise success to land a punchline. Never add a brand sales pitch to the script.

## Review the effect

Does the playful choice help attention, understanding or recall? Does it feel native
to this story rather than pasted onto it? Is its meaning accurate when taken with
the source? If it distracts or trivializes, choose a lighter device. If the script
is a dry paraphrase, improve the scene, voice or visual idea rather than add filler.
