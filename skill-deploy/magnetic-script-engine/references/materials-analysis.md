# Materials analysis

Use these questions when they help you choose material. No worksheet or fixed
analysis order is required. Read as the end viewer, not as a client wanting every
section included.

## Select the critical material

- Select the source passages needed for the chosen takeaway; there is no passage quota.
- Choose the central communication outcome: what should the viewer understand,
  recognize, decide or do after watching? An explanation can end in understanding;
  do not force an action, misconception or behavioral problem into every document.
- Picture the viewer at the moment this information would help: what are they
  looking at, comparing, wondering or deciding? Use that human point of view to
  choose details and explain them, without inventing a biography, symptoms,
  behavior or setting that the source and audience brief do not support.
- Keep a clear takeaway and the shared essential facts behind it. Choose material
  for its value to that outcome, rather than giving every source section airtime.
- Look beyond headings and highlighted boxes for a source detail that makes the
  chosen takeaway easier to picture, understand or apply. A useful fact may be
  buried in a paragraph or table. If it genuinely changes the viewer's understanding,
  consider making it a turning point, explanation or visual reveal. Do not hunt for
  a surprise, impose a passage count, or promote a less prominent detail above the
  source's actual warnings and required actions.
- Use the source's urgency, warnings and emphasis to identify critical content.
  You may choose an editorial order for comprehension; do not imply a new clinical
  priority or change a required procedural sequence.
- When useful, choose a main teaching challenge for the selected job. Describe it as an
  observable moment: what can the viewer notice or do, and what still needs to make
  sense before the next action? A category label alone is not a useful diagnosis.
  Treat this as an editorial hypothesis, not evidence of actual viewer behavior.

Possible internal lenses; choose one only if it helps:

- `False Certainty`: a rule counters a possible incorrect interpretation; do not claim
  that viewers actually hold it without evidence.
- `Cognitive Overload`: the supplied material contains too many competing actions for
  the selected duration.
- `Concept Fragmentation`: source-supplied pieces need clearer organization without a
  new relationship being added.
- `Transfer Failure`: the instruction needs to be turned into an observable action
  sequence; do not invent a history of failed adherence.

Never present an internal diagnosis as a fact about the audience.

## Optional planning lenses

- `job`: the central communication outcome; an observable action when applicable,
  otherwise a specific understanding, recognition or decision.
- `lockedFacts`: exact locked items with source locations.
- `mechanism`: the exact source passage stating why; leave empty if absent.
- `failureAndHook`: one concrete understanding gap, the source detail that resolves
  it, and a moment or question that makes that detail useful. State what changes
  between the opening and the ending: what the viewer can now understand or decide.
  If there is no meaningful gap, use a clear explanation or demonstration without
  manufacturing a barrier or a dramatic correction.
- `cutList`: source material intentionally excluded to fit the supplied budget.
- `audienceFit`: use the supplied profile when available; otherwise address the
  source-supported task in a warm, direct voice without invented demographics.

Red-flag rules for the chosen job must survive every duration. Never pad a sparse
source with generic advice.
