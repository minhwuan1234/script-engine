# Script craft

A document is written to be complete; a script is written to be followed. Rebuild the
source around one observable job instead of narrating every section.

## Shared spine

Both versions use the same source facts and carry this spine:

1. `Tension`: a source-supported decision, contrast, warning or action.
2. `Explanation`: the source-stated reason or mechanism, when one exists.
3. `Action`: one specific thing to do, including its source-stated trigger or timing.

Build tension from a decision, an unanswered source-grounded question or a clearly
hypothetical interpretation the source corrects. Do not assert an audience belief,
behavior or consequence without evidence. If the
source supplies no mechanism, teach the exact rule without adding a reason.

## Choose a storytelling concept before writing

Privately state the viewer's useful takeaway in one sentence, then choose one
organizing idea for each assigned style. A concept specifies how the viewer moves
from the opening to that takeaway: following an action through time, seeing a
source-stated mechanism unfold, comparing stated decision thresholds, or resolving
a meaningful question with sourced evidence. These are options, not a required
menu. Choose the approach the document actually supports.

Build beats from the selected facts to serve the concept; do not follow paragraph
order by default. Each beat should advance understanding or the action. Keep all
locked facts intact while freely rewriting the surrounding language for speech.

Do not create suspense by hiding an ordinary label or checklist action. An opening
such as “What does the handbook explain?” followed by the same question and a list
is a quiz, not a narrative payoff. Q&A can organize genuine decisions, but other
styles should not collapse into a sequence of questions. Sparse sources can still
support a compact action-led demonstration and a differently organized explanation;
use directness rather than padding when they cannot support a reveal.

## Two narrative plans

Before drafting A, privately outline both assigned versions: speaker/viewer
position, opening move, beat sequence, where a source-stated explanation appears,
source-supported memory anchor, closing payoff, and action when provided.
Preserve the same job, locked facts and legitimate takeaway. A misconception can be sourced or a clearly hypothetical interpretation directly
contradicted by the source; never present an editorial hypothesis as audience data.

Aim for meaningful differences in at least two dimensions: perspective, narrative
architecture, mechanism delivery (only when sourced), viewer experience, or memory
anchor. Different titles, synonyms, names or tone alone do not qualify. Work within
the assigned styles; keep the assigned style while using illustrative narration where it helps.
Check a proposed misconception against the exact rule and its conditions. If the
source does not rule it out, choose a different opening. Do not manufacture a false
belief by negating an unrelated fact or by simply adding a question mark.
If the source is too sparse for two supported differences, use the clearest valid
routes and record that limitation in Engine notes. Accuracy takes priority.

## Hook

Apply `references/hook-quality.md` to choose and review the opening. Establish one
useful narrative promise, then let the body develop it. The app supplies a maximum
hook duration of 15 seconds for every video length, with no separate hook word
limit or word quota. Keep the
source-accuracy contract in force for every opening technique.

## Voiceover

- Use one idea per sentence and natural spoken English.
- Mix short emphasis with longer explanatory sentences. Write for a natural breath,
  not a per-sentence word cap. Keep conditions attached to the instruction.
- Prefer concrete nouns and observable verbs already supported by the source.
- Keep locked wording, numbers, units and conditions exact.
- Define a technical term only when the source supplies the definition.
- Do not use `usually`, `typically`, `most people` or similar prevalence language
  unless the source does.
- Cut throat-clearing such as `In this video`, `Let us talk about` and `Did you know`.

## Human voice and visual cooperation

- Keep production decisions out of voiceover. Stage the scene in ON SCREEN; let
  speech address the viewer's task. A natural “Imagine...” can establish a
  hypothetical moment, but never narrate that a scene avoids inventing a reply,
  follows a source rule or has reached an evidence boundary. Omit the unsupported
  detail silently and move to the next useful beat.

- Keep the speaker's viewpoint and knowledge consistent from beat to beat. Use
  first person when compatible with the assigned style. A clearly illustrative
  scene, question or inner dialogue may carry the sourced action; follow the
  illustrative-scene boundaries in source accuracy.
- Introduce information at the moment it helps answer the opening question or
  understand the selected job. Avoid a background dump before its relevance is clear.
- Read the voiceover as speech: vary sentence length, use natural contractions and
  transitions, and preserve technical meaning and all locked conditions. Cut forced
  comedy, synthetic enthusiasm, repetitive questions and generic motivational endings.
- Let visuals explain a source-supported sequence or relationship while voiceover
  guides understanding. Avoid merely reading every on-screen word; repeat exact
  safety instructions or thresholds when that repetition helps comprehension.
- End with the promised payoff and an actionable trigger only when the source
  supplies one. Do not invent a reason, mechanism or when-to-call instruction to
  make the ending stronger.

## Structure

- Privately map a short sequence before writing lines: hook promise → the minimum
  source facts needed to understand the job → the exact action and its trigger.
  Place each locked fact at the beat where the viewer needs it; never detach its
  condition or repeat it only to fill time.
- Give each beat one explanatory job.
- Where source detail allows it, vary the beat about every 5–8 seconds. A new beat
  must contribute a source-supported decision, explanation or action, not padding.
- Pay off any question or contrast opened by the hook.
- An analogy may organize information or illustrate a source-stated relationship.
  Keep the mapping narrow and clear; it must not teach an invented mechanism,
  prediction or recommendation. Use literal explanation when the analogy distorts it.
- Make Versions A and B differ in order, framing and source of tension—not merely in
  wording or character names. Give each version a different source-supported
  opening and route to the same answer. If the material cannot support two distinct
  routes, vary the order of explanation without manufacturing a new premise.
- Let the selected style guide the dominant experience, while freely combining
  storytelling techniques. Its example beats are options, not a checklist. Keep
  the assigned identity and make the two versions recognizably different.
- Follow the app-supplied word and timing budget exactly. Cut background and secondary
  material before cutting locked facts or red-flag rules.
- After drafting, remove any sentence that repeats the preceding beat without
  adding a source-supported clarification, decision or action. Keep useful repetition
  only when it reinforces an exact trigger or prohibition.

## Healthcare shape by duration

Apply this editorial shape when the selected ICP is `healthcare`. The app-supplied
word and timing budget remains authoritative. These are content limits, not a
request to recalculate or override that budget. Both versions cover the same chosen
job and the same essential locked facts; vary the route through them according to
the two assigned styles. Never cut a red-flag rule or its condition for the chosen
job merely to hit a word target. If the source is thin, use the low end of the
budget; if even that requires padding, write shorter and explain the limitation in
`Engine notes`.

### 30–60 seconds

- Cover one job only. Select the two or three highest-priority locked items needed
  for it, plus every source-stated red-flag rule tied to that job. Keep every
  condition attached to the selected items.
- Use one short source-stated mechanism at most. Omit history, background, secondary
  jobs and recap.
- Shape: brief hook within the supplied limit → one core explanation or decision → exact action in the
  last ten seconds.
- For Q&A or Myth-buster, choose the number of questions or contrasts that serves
  the one job; there is no required count.

### 2–3 minutes

- Lead with one job. Include at most two supporting jobs only when the source
  clearly connects them to the main job. Use up to five relevant locked items,
  plus all red-flag rules for the included jobs.
- Shape: hook → source-stated reason or mechanism, if present → what to do in no
  more than three steps → when to call or act, if stated → one-line action recap.
  A clearly illustrative example may make the sourced mechanism concrete without
  adding symptoms, measurements, outcomes or clinical advice.
- For Q&A or Myth-buster, allow connected answers or contrasts to develop without
  enforcing a question count or repeating the same beat pattern.

### Up to 5 minutes

- Include up to three source-supported jobs when there is enough critical content.
  Use up to eight relevant locked items, plus all red-flag rules for those jobs.
  Preserve the source's priority and sequence where changing them could alter
  meaning or imply a new clinical ranking.
- Shape: hook → one beat per job with a one-sentence recap when useful → closing
  checklist of source-stated actions. Include missed-step advice or common
  mistakes only if the source explicitly provides them.
- For Q&A or Myth-buster, use only as many questions or contrasts as the explanation
  needs. Never pad a sparse document to reach a count or the time ceiling.

Across all durations, cut illustrative detail first, then background, then
secondary jobs. Preserve exact wording for numbers, medication or procedural
instructions, prohibitions and `when to call` triggers. A longer duration permits
more source material; it does not permit more assumed facts.

## On-screen direction

Invent the visual treatment, while keeping what it teaches supported by the source. Show the actual
source-stated sequence, comparison, wording or decision when it helps understanding.
Pair voiceover and visuals so the picture clarifies the spoken point; do not simply
repeat every spoken word as text. Use exact on-screen wording for a critical number,
trigger or prohibition when the source provides it. If the source supports no
informative image, use restrained text rather than a fabricated demonstration.
On-screen direction must not introduce an unsupported symptom, reading, anatomy
claim, procedure, result, timeline, setting or cause. Use motion and progression when useful: highlight a stated trigger, build a
source-listed checklist, compare source-provided values, or move between labeled
stages. Layouts, arrows and neutral icons need not appear in the PDF; the relationship
they depict must be supported. Do not invent clinical color zones or measurements.
Avoid decorative icons and filler that teach nothing.

## Ending

End on one observable, source-supported action with its exact trigger or timing. A
callback may be used only if it introduces no new premise. Do not add fake urgency,
reassurance, a brand call to action or a promise of another episode.

## Craft examples: presentation changes, facts stay fixed

These invented teaching examples demonstrate craft only. Do not import their facts
or phrases into an unrelated script.

Source: “Ask your manager when an instruction is unclear.”
Flat: “Employees can ask their manager if an instruction is unclear.”
Scene-led: “Imagine pausing over an instruction: ‘What does this mean?’ That is a
question you can take to your manager.”
Why: the question gives the rule a moment of use without adding a consequence.

Source: “New employees receive the handbook on day one. It explains working hours
and leave requests. During the first week, complete the onboarding checklist and
review it with your manager.”
Flat: repeat that list in the hook, then repeat it again as three answers.
Better route: open on the first-week checklist review; go back to day-one handbook
access; show its two stated topics; return to completing and reviewing the checklist.
Why: a destination gives the sequence shape. Do not invent what is in the checklist.

Before releasing, read the lines as one spoken explanation. Would a person say this,
or does it sound like labels read from a document? Repair stiff transitions and
repetition while leaving locked facts intact. Once a complete action and its timing
have been stated, do not immediately restate them as “That is how...” or a second
summary sentence. Sparse sources may produce a shorter script; never manufacture
length by replaying the same instruction. Creativity is not measured by how
many questions, metaphors or invented details appear.
