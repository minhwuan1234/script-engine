# Script craft

A document is written to be complete; a script is written to be followed. Rebuild the
source around a useful communication outcome instead of narrating every section.

## Narrative movement

Give the viewer a reason to follow the thought from one beat to the next. A
source-supported decision, explanation, contrast or action can supply that movement,
but no particular order or combination is compulsory. Choose the shape that makes
the material clear and memorable. A continuous scene, a visual explanation or a
conversation can work without fitting “tension → explanation → action”.

## Choose a storytelling concept before writing

Privately state the viewer's useful takeaway in one sentence, then choose one
organizing idea for each assigned style. A concept specifies how the viewer moves
from the opening to that takeaway: following an action through time, seeing a
source-stated mechanism unfold, comparing stated decision thresholds, or resolving
a meaningful question with sourced evidence. These are options, not a required
menu. Choose the approach the document actually supports.

Build beats from the selected facts to serve the concept; do not follow paragraph
order by default. Let an important detail answer a question, reveal a relationship,
make a situation recognizable or prepare a decision. Choose what the listener needs
at that moment, rather than linking unrelated facts with “next” or “also”. These are
possible effects, not a mandatory set of beats. Keep locked facts intact while
freely rewriting surrounding language for speech.

Do not create suspense by hiding an ordinary label or checklist action. An opening
such as “What does the handbook explain?” followed by the same question and a list
is a quiz, not a narrative payoff. Q&A can organize genuine decisions, but other
styles should not collapse into a sequence of questions. Sparse sources can still
support a compact action-led demonstration and a differently organized explanation;
use directness rather than padding when they cannot support a reveal.

## Make the information do something

Use the chosen takeaway and any useful understanding gap to give the script a
throughline. Ask why this viewer would need the next line now. A question, observation or
unfinished action should lead into the relevant fact; that fact should change what
the viewer understands, notices or does next. This is a writing test, not a formula
that every beat must announce.

For a journey, let a source-supported rule enter at the moment the character needs
it. For an explainer, let a detail or demonstration create the need for the next
connection. The source supplies the reason behind a rule when available. When it
does not, show how to apply the instruction without inventing a clinical “because”.

Carry one question, action or visual thread far enough to develop it, instead of
resetting the story at every timestamp. Return to it when that gives the ending
meaning: the same thing is seen differently or the next step is now clear. A callback
is a consequence of the explanation, not an obligatory repeated line. Do not force
a real-world success, belief or outcome just to close the loop.

## Two narrative plans

Develop an independent creative concept for each assigned style, sharing one
communication objective and the same selected locked facts. Before releasing A,
know enough about both concepts to see how the viewing experiences will differ.
B should grow from its own concept, rather than come from rephrasing A after it is
written. A and B labels do not determine narrator or perspective; an explicit
first-person style still calls for its own appropriate point of view.

Perspective, opening, beat order, explanation, visual motif and ending are choices,
not fields to fill or a required outline. Ask how the viewer will encounter and
remember the material in each version. Each script should be worth watching on its
own. Preserve the shared objective and legitimate takeaway. A misconception can be
sourced or a clearly hypothetical interpretation directly contradicted by the source; never present an editorial hypothesis as audience data.

Aim for meaningful differences in at least two dimensions: perspective, narrative
architecture, mechanism delivery (only when sourced), viewer experience, or memory
anchor. Different titles, synonyms, names or tone alone do not qualify. Work within
the assigned styles; keep the assigned style while using illustrative narration where it helps.
Check a proposed misconception against the exact rule and its conditions. If the
source does not rule it out, choose a different opening. Do not manufacture a false
belief by negating an unrelated fact or by simply adding a question mark.
If the source is too sparse for two supported differences, use the clearest valid
routes and record that limitation in Engine notes. Accuracy takes priority.

useful narrative promise, then let the body develop it. The app supplies a maximum
hook duration of 15 seconds for every video length, with no separate hook word
limit or word quota. Keep the

## Spoken voice and visual cooperation

Use commas, periods, colons or parentheses instead of long dash punctuation. Rewrite
around the pause naturally; do not use em dashes or en dashes in the deliverable.
Keep an ordinary short hyphen for ranges such as timestamps.

Write words for a person to say to someone, not for a narrator describing a PDF.
Choose a speaker, a listener and what the speaker is trying to help them notice or
do. Let that relationship govern the language. Use contractions, natural fragments,
short reactions and longer explanations as the thought requires. There is no
per-sentence word quota. Keep exact locked wording within otherwise flexible speech.

Begin inside the subject: a task, an object, a question or a moment. Describe the
source document only when the document itself is the thing the viewer needs to use.
“The guide mentions the manager twice” explains a writer's observation; it does not
give the viewer a reason to care. Move to the actual situation the rule addresses.
“Working hours are in the handbook” can be useful; “the source establishes two
separate information channels” sounds like analysis and belongs outside voiceover.

Write the spoken thought before fitting it into timestamp blocks. Group speech by
meaningful beats; do not add a stiff transition just to start a new block. Let the
next thought grow out of the previous one: a question needs an answer, an observation
needs its meaning, or a decision needs its action. Avoid counting facts, announcing
structure or manufacturing a contrast between unrelated instructions.

When first person suits the style, a voice can hesitate, wonder or make a small
observation inside an illustrative moment. Establish fictional framing in ON SCREEN
when needed; every script need not start “Imagine I am...”. Do not manufacture a
biography, testimonial, symptoms or outcomes. Explain clinical or technical terms
only as far as the source supports.

Use `references/tone-of-voice.md` for connected, conversational explanation and
light wit when it serves the thought. Give sourced reasoning room to develop.
Let the picture do the staging so the voice can speak naturally. Do
not read stage directions, evidence limits or editing decisions to the viewer.

Before releasing a version, privately read just its voiceover as continuous speech.
Would a person say these words in that situation? Repair bureaucratic nouns,
explanatory scaffolding and repeated instructions. If a playful line needs a setup
longer than the idea it teaches, simplify it. Keep a plain reading of every critical
condition, number or urgent instruction. A short, honest piece beats padded banter.

## Structure

- Place information where it helps the viewer understand the thought or action.
  Discover the sequence through the approach that works for the piece; there is
  no required outline. Keep conditions attached and avoid repetition for padding.
- Give each beat one explanatory job.
- Where source detail allows it, vary the beat about every 5–8 seconds. A new beat
  must contribute a source-supported decision, explanation or action, not padding.
- Pay off any question or contrast opened by the hook.
- An analogy may organize information or illustrate a source-stated relationship.
  Keep the mapping narrow and clear; it must not teach an invented mechanism,
  prediction or recommendation. Use literal explanation when the analogy distorts it.
- Make Versions A and B differ in order, framing and source of tension—not merely in
  wording or character names. Give each version a different source-supported
  opening and route to the same answer. If the material cannot support two distinct
  routes, vary the order of explanation without manufacturing a new premise.
- Let the selected style guide the dominant experience, while freely combining
  storytelling techniques. Its example beats are options, not a checklist. Keep
  the assigned identity and make the two versions recognizably different.
- Follow the app-supplied word and timing budget exactly. Cut background and secondary
  material before cutting locked facts or red-flag rules.
- After drafting, remove any sentence that repeats the preceding beat without
  adding a source-supported clarification, decision or action. Keep useful repetition
  only when it reinforces an exact trigger or prohibition.

## Healthcare scope and duration

Let the duration influence how much material the viewer can absorb, not dictate
the plot. A 30–60-second piece usually needs one focused job; a longer piece can
support connected tasks or a fuller explanation when the source warrants it.
Choose the number of beats, questions, examples and returns to the opening freely.
No fixed step count, timestamp for a reveal or mandatory recap applies.

Keep the source-stated red-flag rules for the selected job, including every
condition. Do not replace them with a joke or cut them to make room for background.
When the document gives a mechanism, choose how to make it understandable; when it
does not, demonstrate the action without inventing a clinical reason. Preserve
source-required procedural sequences inside whatever narrative structure you choose.

Fit the app-supplied duration and word budget with natural pacing. Cut secondary
material rather than rush locked instructions. If the source cannot support the
requested length without padding, write a shorter complete piece and state why in
Engine notes. The hook remains at most 15 seconds with no separate word limit.

## On-screen direction

Invent the visual treatment, while keeping what it teaches supported by the source. Show the actual
source-stated sequence, comparison, wording or decision when it helps understanding.
Pair voiceover and visuals so the picture clarifies the spoken point; do not simply
repeat every spoken word as text. Use exact on-screen wording for a critical number,
trigger or prohibition when the source provides it. If the source supports no
informative image, use restrained text rather than a fabricated demonstration.
On-screen direction must not introduce an unsupported symptom, reading, anatomy
claim, procedure, result, timeline, setting or cause. Use motion and progression when useful: highlight a stated trigger, build a
source-listed checklist, compare source-provided values, or move between labeled
stages. Layouts, arrows and neutral icons need not appear in the PDF; the relationship
they depict must be supported. Do not invent clinical color zones or measurements.
Avoid decorative icons and filler that teach nothing.

## Ending

End where the thought feels complete: a useful sourced action, a clarified
distinction or an image whose meaning has developed. Include an action and its
trigger when the chosen job requires one. A callback is available, not mandatory.
Do not invent urgency, reassurance, a sales CTA or a promise of another episode.

## Small examples of spoken craft

These invented examples illustrate transformations, not facts to reuse elsewhere.

Source: “Return the device to its charging dock after use.”
Stiff: “The document specifies a post-use device-return requirement.”
Spoken: “Finished using it? Back to the charging dock.”
Visual option: the dock appears as a little parking bay. Keep the real return
instruction clear; the parking image is a metaphor, not a new device feature.

Source: “When an instruction is unclear, ask your manager.”
Stiff: “This is the second occasion on which the manager appears.”
Spoken: “Hang on. What does that instruction mean? That's a question for my manager.”
Why: the rule enters when the speaker needs it; no invented answer or result follows.

A useful opening starts the thought; the body develops it. Do not repeat all of the
opening's instructions in the next beats. The closing should complete the thought
or reinforce its useful action, not explain that the narrative is now complete.
